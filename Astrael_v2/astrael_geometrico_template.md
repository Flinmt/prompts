## Geometric Construction and Shape Hierarchy

Build the painting at three scales:

1. **Primary masses:** Establish the silhouette, head, torso, limbs, major garments, and overall light-versus-shadow arrangement. These must remain readable at thumbnail size.
2. **Secondary planes:** Clarify changes in orientation, important folds, facial structure, joints, and overlapping forms. Concentrate them where they improve expression, shading, or material separation.
3. **Selective accents:** Resolve eyes, mouth, a meaningful hand gesture, a fastening, or an equipment mark. Keep their contrast and density subordinate to the focal hierarchy.

Do not impose a fixed polygon count on a face, limb, or garment. Use enough planes to achieve the reference's painterly richness while preserving the large groups. A major garment mass may contain several secondary shadows without becoming a separate collection of objects.

Vary plane size, angle, and contour. Long tapered shapes, broad irregular quadrilaterals, wedges, and occasional smaller angular patches should follow the subject's structure. Avoid equal-sized triangular tessellation, decorative faceting, and evenly distributed detail.

The geometry must organize the painting from the start. Skin and facial anatomy share the same angular language as the clothing. Suggest depth with painted planes and overlaps while maintaining an authored 2D appearance.

## Lighting and Layered Painterly Shadows

Use one principal environmental light source with a coherent direction across the face, torso, arms, clothing, equipment, and background. Its narrative source may change between scenes; retain the shared warm-light and cool-shadow relationship.

### Value Families

Organize the painting into a light family, a midtone family, and a shadow family, supported by selective deep accents. These are broad value groups, not a restriction to three literal colors.

Allow restrained value and temperature variations within each family. They should reveal secondary planes without breaking the overall light-and-shadow separation. A painted patch inside shadow remains recognizably part of shadow; do not turn every plane change into a highlight.

Keep the face's key features at the strongest local contrast. Support them with readable head and torso midtones. Darker lower garments and peripheral masses may anchor the image, but do not bury the character's construction in black.

### Shadow Structure

* **Primary form shadow:** Establish a broad, connected shadow group on planes turning away from the light. Keep its silhouette intentional and expressive.
* **Secondary form planes:** Add selected darker or lighter painted shapes within that group to describe cheek structure, the turn of an arm, a folded sleeve, or a garment bending around the torso.
* **Cast shadows:** Show purposeful shadows where one form blocks the light: hair on forehead or cheek, chin on neck, collar on chest, sleeve on arm, a hand on clothing, or a belt over trousers. Use only relationships actually present in the specified pose.
* **Contact accents:** Place small, deep shadows at important close overlaps and recesses. Avoid an automatic dark outline around every object or joint.

Shadow richness comes from the design of these shapes, their relative values, and their placement. Keep asymmetrical cuts and secondary planes where they explain the subject; leave other areas broad and quiet.

### Dark-Area Readability

Preserve differences among hair, fabric, leather, and background even when all are dark. Use restrained shifts in value, temperature, edge, and plane direction. The shared cool shadow family may contain multiple related mixtures suited to each local material and skin tone.

A subtle environmental influence may appear as a muted painted plane inside shadow, but it must not create a second dramatic light source, a bright rim, or a photographic bounce-light simulation.

### Shadow Edges

Use decisive edges for selected cast shadows and close contacts, more painterly edges for broad form turns, and limited soft transitions where adjacent planes need integration. Do not make every shadow razor-sharp or equally soft. Preserve visible plane boundaries across the image.

Avoid uniform dark fills, smooth airbrushed modeling, glossy facial highlights, theatrical backlighting, multiple colored lights, bloom, and scattered tiny highlights. More elaborate shadows must not turn into photographic rendering or a dense polygon mosaic.

## Face, Skin, and Expression

The face receives the richest controlled plane relationships and the most precise feature placement. Preserve the identity described in the character data, including age, asymmetry, bone structure, and expression.

Establish broad forehead, cheek, nose, jaw, and neck relationships, then add secondary planes around the eyes, cheek turns, nose underside, lips, and hair contact where useful. Keep these shapes large enough to read as painted decisions rather than skin texture.

* **Eyes:** Use clear upper lids, restrained lower lids, simple irises, and small highlights when supported by the light. Preserve the specified eye color. Eye sockets may receive layered angular shadows without becoming realistically modeled cavities.

## Material Separation

Distinguish materials through painted value ranges, temperature, edge behavior, fold design, and selective marks:

* **Skin:** Warm midtones, related cool shadows, angular secondary planes, matte restrained highlights.
* **Wool and heavy fabric:** Broad matte folds, substantial shadow wedges, softly broken internal edges.
* **Linen and lighter cloth:** Narrower fold rhythms and lighter planes without rendering every wrinkle.
* **Leather:** More defined plane breaks, compressed folds, and a few muted broken highlights.
* **Fine or waxed fabric:** Restrained directional sheen contained within broad planes; no glossy photographic finish.
* **Dark fabric:** Several related charcoal and blue-grey values that keep folds and overlaps readable.
* **Metal:** Tarnished brass, oxidized silver, dull steel, blackened iron, or smoked bronze in small accents. A few sharper light-dark breaks identify the material.
* **Transparent fabric or glass, when specified:** A simplified underlying value showing through and limited edge cues. Do not introduce a detailed optical simulation.
* **Stone, plaster, and wood:** Broader, quieter planes with minimal local variation in the environment.

Visible painterly modulation is welcome where it clarifies a surface. Avoid all-over grain, procedural noise, realistic pores, dense fabric weave, chrome reflections, and repeated material texture.

## Brushwork and Edge Hierarchy

Use predominantly opaque block painting with broad and medium brush shapes, supported by smaller deliberate marks in the focal areas. Brush direction should follow anatomy, hair flow, cloth tension, or structural orientation.

Permit localized dry-brush variation, slight overlap of paint shapes, and small irregularities along selected edges. These should resemble intentional paint application, not a distressed filter over the entire image.

* **Sharp or semi-sharp:** Eyes, selected nose and mouth boundaries, important cast-shadow cuts, close overlaps, and meaningful equipment edges.
* **Controlled painterly:** Most skin planes, hair divisions, garment folds, hands, and silhouette turns.
* **Soft, broken, or lost:** Selected form transitions, peripheral hair and clothing, distant structure, and atmosphere.

Vary these edges across both light and shadow. A limited soft transition can integrate two planes without erasing their angular construction. Keep the most precise edge relationships near the face; do not spread equal sharpness over all clothing and scenery.

Finish means deliberate shape closure, readable overlaps, coherent shading, and selective elaboration. Avoid thick impasto, uncontrolled sketchiness, vector-perfect contours, uniform comic outlines, and continuous airbrush gradients.
